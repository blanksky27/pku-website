A guide for parents of newborns

PKU: what new parents need to know

PKU is a rare condition that babies are born with. A baby with PKU often looks healthy at birth, but without early treatment it can harm the developing brain. A simple newborn blood test finds it early, and with the right care, serious harm can be prevented.

PKU in plain words

Phenylketonuria (PKU) means the body cannot break down a protein building block called phenylalanine (“Phe” for short). It is found in protein foods, including breast milk and formula. In babies with PKU, Phe slowly builds up in the blood.

Why it matters

Too much Phe can affect how a baby’s brain develops. Without treatment, it can lead to developmental delay, intellectual disability and seizures, and some damage cannot be reversed. That is why finding PKU early makes such a difference.

Why it happens

PKU is passed down in families. It is no one’s fault. The next two sections explain the cause and what goes wrong inside the body.

Looking healthy doesn’t rule it out. A baby with PKU may look completely well at birth. That is why newborns are tested even when everything seems fine.

What causes PKU

PKU is caused by changes (variants) in a gene called PAH. This gene holds the instructions for making an enzyme, a protein that speeds up one specific chemical reaction. This enzyme is called phenylalanine hydroxylase, or PAH too.

Two copies, one from each parent

Everyone inherits two copies of the PAH gene. PKU is autosomal recessive: a baby is affected only when both copies carry a harmful change. A parent with one changed copy is a “carrier”. Their working copy makes enough enzyme, so carriers are usually healthy and often unaware.

A change can mean too little enzyme is made, or that the enzyme works poorly. How much activity is left decides how severe PKU is.

How PKU affects the body

To understand PKU, follow phenylalanine (Phe) from food to the brain. Switch between a healthy baby and a baby with PKU to compare.

  1. Food proteinDigested into amino acids, including Phe.
  2. Phe in the bloodStays within a safe range.Builds up to high levels.
  3. Liver cellsPAH enzyme, with its helper BH₄, converts Phe to tyrosine.PAH missing or weak, so Phe is not converted. Side products also form.
  4. TyrosinePlenty available.Supply falls.
  5. Brain cellsMake dopamine and norepinephrine normally.Fewer brain messengers; development disrupted.

  1. Liver cells

    In a healthy babyLiver cells (hepatocytes) make the PAH enzyme. Phe is an essential amino acid used to build proteins, and PAH removes the surplus by turning it into tyrosine. This keeps Phe in a narrow, safe range.

    In PKUWithout working PAH, surplus Phe stays in the blood. Some is pushed down a minor side route, making products such as phenylpyruvate (“phenylketones”, which give PKU its name) that also build up.

  2. Blood-brain barrier

    In a healthy babyCells lining the brain’s blood vessels use a carrier called LAT1 to shuttle large amino acids, including Phe, tyrosine and tryptophan, into the brain in balance.

    In PKUVery high Phe floods the carrier and crowds out the others. More Phe enters the brain, and less tyrosine and tryptophan.

  3. Brain cells

    In a healthy babyNeurons use tyrosine to make dopamine and norepinephrine, and tryptophan to make serotonin. These chemical messengers pass signals between cells. Oligodendrocytes wrap nerve fibres in myelin so signals travel fast.

    In PKULess raw material, plus high Phe interfering with the enzymes that make messengers, lowers their levels. Protein building and myelin are also disturbed. A baby’s brain is wiring itself rapidly, so this can cause developmental delay, intellectual disability and seizures.

Why babies look fine at first. Before birth, the mother’s liver clears the baby’s extra Phe. After birth, Phe climbs as milk feeds begin. That is why the test is ideally done at 24–72 hours, and why treatment that lowers Phe protects the brain.

Phenylalanine (Phe)
An amino acid in protein foods. The body needs some, but not too much.
Tyrosine
An amino acid made from Phe. Used for proteins, pigment and brain messengers.
PAH enzyme
The liver enzyme that converts Phe to tyrosine. It needs the helper molecule BH₄ (a cofactor).
Neurotransmitter
A chemical messenger that carries signals between nerve cells.
Myelin
The fatty insulation around nerve fibres that speeds up signals.

What happens next

There are three stages. Most families only need to think about the first one.

  1. 1

    The newborn test

    A nurse pricks your baby’s heel and collects a few drops of blood on a special card. It is quick, and it screens for PKU and other rare conditions.

    Ideally 24–72 hours after birth
  2. 2

    Check the result

    If the result is out of range, your baby needs more blood tests to confirm. Book these promptly and see the specialist team.

    Act quickly, but stay calm
  3. 3

    Start care

    If PKU is confirmed, a specialist team builds a care plan for your baby and teaches you how to follow it.

    Ongoing support

An out-of-range result is not a diagnosis. It only means more testing is needed to be sure.

Caring for a baby with PKU

If your baby is diagnosed, you won’t be doing this alone. Care is built around three things.

A specialist team

Doctors and dietitians who set up your baby’s plan and answer your questions.

A special diet

Your baby still needs some Phe to grow, so it is limited to a target set for your baby, not removed completely. A medical formula provides the other nutrients.

Regular blood tests

These check your baby’s Phe level so the team can adjust the plan as your baby grows.

Please don’t change your baby’s feeding on your own. Always follow the plan from the specialist team, and ask them if you’re unsure about anything, including breastfeeding.

Common questions

Did I do something to cause this?

No. PKU is inherited, and nothing you did or didn’t do caused it.

Will my baby look unwell at birth?

Not necessarily. A baby may look completely healthy, which is why screening matters. Symptoms may not be obvious before high Phe levels start to affect development.

Does an out-of-range result mean my baby has PKU?

No. It means further tests are needed. Confirmatory blood tests and a specialist review are how PKU is diagnosed, so go to those appointments promptly.

Will my baby have to avoid protein completely?

No. The body needs some Phe. Treatment keeps it within a target set for your baby by the specialist team.

Is treatment for life?

Usually, yes. Care continues with controlled Phe intake, medical nutrition and regular blood tests, and your team will guide you at each stage.

Could it happen again in a future pregnancy?

If both parents carry the changed gene, each pregnancy has a 1 in 4 chance of a baby with PKU. A genetic counsellor can explain what this means for your family.

How common is PKU?

It is rare. Worldwide, it affects roughly 1 in 24,000 babies on average, though this varies from country to country.
