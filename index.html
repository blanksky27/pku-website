PKU: what new parents need to know

PKU is a rare condition that babies are born with. A baby with PKU often looks healthy at birth, but without early treatment it can harm the developing brain. A simple newborn blood test finds it early, and with the right care, serious harm can be prevented.

PKU in plain words

Phenylketonuria (PKU) means the body cannot break down a protein building block called phenylalanine (“Phe” for short). It is found in protein foods, including breast milk and formula. In babies with PKU, Phe slowly builds up in the blood.

Why it matters

Too much Phe can affect how a baby’s brain develops. Without treatment, it can lead to developmental delay, intellectual disability and seizures, and some damage cannot be reversed. That is why finding PKU early makes such a difference.

Why it happens

PKU is passed down in families. A baby has it when both parents carry a changed copy of the same gene, usually without knowing. It is no one’s fault.

Looking healthy doesn’t rule it out. A baby with PKU may look completely well at birth. That is why newborns are tested even when everything seems fine.

What happens next

There are three stages. Most families only need to think about the first one.

  1. 1

    The newborn test

    A nurse pricks your baby’s heel and collects a few drops of blood on a special card. It is quick, and it screens for PKU and other rare conditions.

    Ideally 24–72 hours after birth
  2. 2

    Check the result

    If the result is out of range, your baby needs more blood tests to confirm. Book these promptly and see the specialist team.

    Act quickly, but stay calm
  3. 3

    Start care

    If PKU is confirmed, a specialist team builds a care plan for your baby and teaches you how to follow it.

    Ongoing support

An out-of-range result is not a diagnosis. It only means more testing is needed to be sure.

Caring for a baby with PKU

If your baby is diagnosed, you won’t be doing this alone. Care is built around three things.

A specialist team

Doctors and dietitians who set up your baby’s plan and answer your questions.

A special diet

Your baby still needs some Phe to grow, so it is limited to a target set for your baby, not removed completely. A medical formula provides the other nutrients.

Regular blood tests

These check your baby’s Phe level so the team can adjust the plan as your baby grows.

Please don’t change your baby’s feeding on your own. Always follow the plan from the specialist team, and ask them if you’re unsure about anything, including breastfeeding.

Common questions

Did I do something to cause this?

No. PKU is inherited, and nothing you did or didn’t do caused it.

Will my baby look unwell at birth?

Not necessarily. A baby may look completely healthy, which is why screening matters. Symptoms may not be obvious before high Phe levels start to affect development.

Does an out-of-range result mean my baby has PKU?

No. It means further tests are needed. Confirmatory blood tests and a specialist review are how PKU is diagnosed, so go to those appointments promptly.

Will my baby have to avoid protein completely?

No. The body needs some Phe. Treatment keeps it within a target set for your baby by the specialist team.

Is treatment for life?

Usually, yes. Care continues with controlled Phe intake, medical nutrition and regular blood tests, and your team will guide you at each stage.

Could it happen again in a future pregnancy?

If both parents carry the changed gene, each pregnancy has a 1 in 4 chance of a baby with PKU. A genetic counsellor can explain what this means for your family.

How common is PKU?

It is rare. Worldwide, it affects roughly 1 in 24,000 babies on average, though this varies from country to country.
